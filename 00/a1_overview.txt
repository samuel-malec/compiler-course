## Overview

Most programmers spend their whole careers on the far side of a
compiler: they write text, something turns it into a running
program, and the details of that ‘something’ remain a mystery. The
goal of this course is to remove the mystery. By the end of the
semester, you will have written (in small, testable pieces) every
major part of a language implementation: a lexer, a parser, a
tree-walking interpreter, a static type checker, a bytecode compiler
with a virtual machine, a simple optimiser and a garbage collector.

The course is, first and foremost, a «programming» course. The
theory (grammars, semantics, data-flow analysis) is there to make
the programs possible, not the other way around. Each chapter
explains just enough of it to get you going and then asks you to
write code – a lot of code. It is important that you program as
much and as often as possible, ideally every day, but at least a few
times each week.

### Topics

The course is split into 3 blocks. Each block contains 4 chapters
and ends with a review chapter. Each block also comes with an
assignment set, which is considerably more extensive than the weekly
exercises.

### The Running Example

Most exercises are standalone – each file defines its own little
language, so that you can solve it without having solved anything
else. However, the demos in each chapter gradually build up a
single language called «tiny», and the assignment sets reuse it as
well. By the end of block 1, tiny has integers, booleans, strings,
‹let› bindings and conditionals; by the end of block 2, it has
statements, first-class functions and a static type system; and in
block 3, it is compiled to bytecode instead of being interpreted.

### Pacing

There are no deadlines, but the material is written for a pace of
one chapter per week, with the assignment set of each block spread
over the 4 weeks of that block. A sensible weekly routine looks like
this:

 1. read ‹00_intro.txt› and all the demos in the chapter, and run
    them (‹make demo›),
 2. solve the elementary exercises (‹e*›) and compare your
    solutions with the reference ones in ‹sol›,
 3. solve at least 4 of the 6 preparatory exercises (‹p*›) «without
    looking at the reference solutions»,
 4. work through the regular exercises (‹r*›), which are somewhat
    harder and often combine ideas from the whole chapter,
 5. put in a couple of hours on the current assignment set.

If you get stuck on an exercise for more than an hour, move on to
the next one and come back to it later. The worst thing you can do
is to fall behind and then try to cram 4 chapters into one weekend.
